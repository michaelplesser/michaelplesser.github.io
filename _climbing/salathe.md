I climbed the Salathe wall with my old friend Alex Crist, but a finger infection complicated things...

# Road to el Cap

Considering that for most of my life I was a boulderer, the fact that I'm climbing big walls may seem a bit odd. 
In fact, there is a very clear path that led me to el Cap. 

I finished undergrad in **2019** and after 3 months in South Africa, came back the US in **September**. Living with my brother and working at the climbing gym as a routesetter and coach, I worked on my PhD applications which go out in mid-**December**. In early **2020**, COVID began to creep on the scene, closing the climbing gym and leaving me unemployed. In early **March**, while coming back from a bouldering competition in VA (where I got 2nd) my car got hit and run on the highway and was totaled. Within a week I'd bought a 2013 Ford Transit Connect van and started converting it to have a bed, lights, solar, etc. In **May**, I hit the road, heading west.

My plan was to go to Wyoming. In the Wind River Range there are remote climbing areas where you can stay off the grid and see more coyotes than people. Eventually I moved on to Seattle to meet up with my brother, fix up *his* van, and then road trip and rock climb wherever we could. While there, I got my first PhD acceptance to Penn State, which I ultimately accepted! The rest of the trip had a distinctly celebratory and transitory mood. Around **July**, having won the lottery for Yosemite reservations, me and my brother took our vans and went south. We met up with my friends from undergrad, Alex and Lucy, and as two teams we climbed Snake Dyke on Half Dome together, along with a number of other smaller walls. 

At the end of two weeks in Yosemite climbing, living in our vans, and generally reveling in the freedom and beauty that defines the American West, I was thoroughly intoxicated. At the same time, being at the cusp of beginning my PhD I knew such freedom wouldn't last long, and that other things would soon take focus. This was good. I wanted to shift focus from climbing to physics, but it was a shift nonetheless. On the last day, standing in el Cap meadow, staring up at el Cap and seeing the headlamps of climbers flick on as the light faded, I imagined myself up there. This is the first time I seriously considered climbing el Cap. Bouldering fitness is hard to maintain while doing a PhD, but maybe big walls were another thing altogether...


![Picture of our headlamps on the wall](/images/Salathe/headlamps.jpg)

Fast forward to **2025**. After a few summers consumed by conference travel, I finally had a gap in my schedule May/June where I could go out west. I called up Alex who was immediately in, and started discussing routes, dates, logistics, and the other million things that go into big walling. Alex had climbed the RNW of Half Dome and the Nose of el Cap already, so we settled on Salathe, hoping the opportunity for free climbing would suit me. The climb has a fearsome reputation for lots of dangerous / intimidating wide climbing, which is well-deserved. 

In **May 2026**, my wife Elena and I set out in the van for her to see the American West via the classic great American road trip. After winding through South Dakota and Wyoming, we eventually arrived in Yosemite. Elena and I had a few chill days in Yosemite (her first time) sightseeing and doing some light climbing because once Alex arrived we would just have a few days to prepare and set off.

My birthday was May 29th. On May 30th, Alex arrived. On May 31st, we packed our bags. On June 1st, we pre-hauled to heart ledges. On June 3rd, we started climbing. On June 5th, we summited. On June 6th, I was in surgery...?

# Trip Report

## The Route:

The Salathe Wall is the second most famous route on el Capitan, after the Nose. It is a massive 35 pitches, about 3500' or 1070 meters. It was first **aid** climbed in 1961, and was later the first route on el Cap to be **free** climbed in 1988. This route overlaps ~85% with the route Freerider, which is the route that Alex Honnold famously **free soloed**. To be clear of the differences,
*	**Aid Climbing:** gear used to assist in ascending the wall *and* for protection,
*	**Free Climbing:** gear used solely for protection,
*	**Free Solo Climbing:** no gear used for either assistance or for safety.

Most people climb the Salathe by a mix of aid and free climbing, as did we.

![Salathe to el Cap Spire Route Overlay](/images/Salathe/route-overlay.jpg)

## The Climb:

### Packing

On May 31st we sat in the Camp 4 parking lot and packed.

Turns out it takes a **LOT** of gear to spend 4 days on the wall, which is what we were planning for.
![Gear explosion](/images/Salathe/gear-spread.jpg)
We brought 8 gallons of water, food rations for 4.5 days (two people), basically a triple rack of cams from 0.1-#2, doubles in #3-4, a #5, a #6, and a #7 (which is enormous but also proved to be essential). We also had a portaledge to sleep on, although the route featured so many ledges that I ended up sleeping on the ground and leaving Alex the ledge to himself. They get pretty tippy with more than one person in them!

### Pre-hauling to Heart Ledges

Because the climb starts with low-angle traversing climbing, the common strategy is to pre-haul your heavy bags to Heart Ledges and meet up with them near the end of day 1. We prehauled on June 1st, which involves going up a set of fixed ropes. A note about those ropes is that some of them are in pretty terrible condition! At most stations there are two ropes to pick from, and halfway up you may discover that the rope you picked is fraying, full of knots, or is so sun-baked and stiff if won't go through an ascender. On the way down at least you know which ropes are reliable...


![Picture of us hauling, taken from the meadow through binoculars](/images/Salathe/hauling.jpg)

 We had a large and medium haul bag, a bag for the ledge, and a waste case (AKA a poop bag). We hauled 1-1 and quickly realized this was a mistake. I had been talking crap about the 2-1 pulley for hauling, thinking that losses to friction would make the realistic advantage not worth the hassle, but I was **wrong**! We managed to haul up to Hearts with great effort, and as soon as we were on the ground called around to borrow a 2-1 pulley system. Luckily, my other good friend from undergrad Carlin works as a climbing guide in Yosemite, and was a source of much gear, knowledge, and support.

 Back on the ground we were feeling more tired than we would have liked. It was decided an extra rest day before we start climbing would be a good idea, so the next day we rested.

### The Rest Day and the Infamous Nut Tool

On June 2nd we went around gathering any last supplies we needed like extra headlamp batteries. Me, Elena, Alex, and Carlin would meet by the Merced river in el Cap Meadow for a dinner picnic. During the afternoon, we realized that neither me nor Alex could find our nut tool -- a thin metal shim used to remove stuck gear from cracks. While not essential, we did want to bring one if we could. I volunteered to improvise one from a butter knife. All I had to do was use a power drill I had in the van to add a small hole so that we could clip the tool to our harnesses and not drop it. While inspecting my work, I ran my hand over a part of the handle where there was a metal burr, and got a shallow 1cm-long cut near the tip of my right index finger. I immediately cursed my carelessness, knowing I would have to climb with tape on my finger now which would be annoying. Whatever. I washed the wound, used some anti-biotic ointment, put a band-aid on it, and forgot about it. I went back to the meadow to prepare for our picnic.

### Climbing Day 1

On June 3rd early in the pre-dawn hours, we started climbing.

![View from the base of the route](/images/Salathe/el-cap-base-view.jpg)

Alex took the first ~5 pitches in blocks. We shortfixed to move faster, meaning that once at the top of a pitch he would fix my rope and self belay himself so he could already begin the next pitch. He dropped one of my alien cams on the second pitch, but another party coming up behind to attempt the route Triple Direct kindly returned the dropped piece to us. I navigated the next ~5 pitches, some fun sporty slab climbing, a slippery chimney, and then two pitches of easy 5.8 free climbing to bring us to Mammoth terraces. Walking along the ledge using a fixed-rope hand-rail, we rapped down to Heart to meet up with our bags. We were making great time. We thought about maybe sleeping on Heart ledges, but with hours of daylight left we decided to keep going. The only concern was that the sun was strong, causing us to drink more water than we had anticipated. Nevertheless, things looked good.

After a break, we continued on. A few ledgey pitches lead to the hollow flake, a 100' long chasm that slowly widens from 8" to 16". To climb it, one lowers down, uses the rope as a pendulum to swing over to the crack, and then wedges into it facing out (back-to-the wall). You can't put in any protection because the friction from the rope would be too great. You can put one piece of protection in above you and bump it upwards as you move, but if that piece fails you would face broken legs at the minimum. Alex stepped up and dispatched it cooly, one of the scarier and cooler bits of climbing I've seen live! I congratulated him on the radio, as did Elena who was watching through binoculars from the meadow down below with her own radio. Around that time we were passed by a climber descending. They told us there was "a lot of water" stashed up at the Alcove from climbers who bailed last week because of a storm. "Like, 8 gallons". Good to know, our water rations rapidly depleting... 

![The view of Cathedral across the valley.](/images/Salathe/cathedral-view.jpg)

This brought us to our bed for the night, Hollow Flake Ledge. It was surprisingly comfortable. I found a flat spot between a rock and the wall to sleep in, and Alex pitched the portaledge above me. We did lose a sandwich to a mouse, but so it goes. After pitching camp, Alex surprised me by getting inspired to climb one more pitch, a reputedly stout chimney above the ledge, to fix the rope and allow us to skip past it in the morning. We got our gear back on and he set off into the dark. As often happens on low-grade chimneys in Yosemite, he got hammered. It was a pitch where you can choose to have easy climbing or to have protection, but not both. Alex opted for protection and paid the price, barely getting through the alleged "5.7". 

Around 9:30 a pair of climbers came up to the ledge attempting to do the Salathe in a single 24 hour push, climbing through the night. They were free climbing and openly bragging about how little gear they were bringing for the sake of moving quickly. After a short stop on our ledge they passed us, demonstrating for us how easy the chimney is if you simply don't use any protection. A few hours later I was awoken to some headlamps on the ledge. I was groggy and didn't fully wake up, but heard from Alex the next morning that one of those climbers took a big fall and twisted his ankle 7 pitches above us, and they had to turn back and retreat. 

### Climbing Day 2

We woke up early, around 4 AM. My index finger was a bit sore. I cleaned my hands with hand sanitizer, re-applied some ointment, and wrapped it up tightly. Though I couldn't have known at the time, this was the first sign of things starting to go wrong.

Nevertheless, we made good progress, reaching the Alcove ahead of schedule.

We originally thought about sleeping in the Alcove, or maybe on the Spire, but at the rate we were drinking water we didn't have enough for a 4 day push as planned. In the alcove we only found 1.5 gallons of water, not 8 as advertised, and it wasn't clear if it was abandoned or not. The higher we went, the harder retreat would become. Eventually we decided to try for a 3 day push since we were ahead of schedule for the second day in a row, and hoped we might find more water stashed higher on the route if needs be. We continued on past the el Cap spire.

![View from above the Spire](/images/Salathe/spire-landscape.jpg)

The rest of the climbing was less fun. We wanted to reach the Block to sleep, a large sloping ledge around pitch 25 or so. Right before that we had the sewer, a perpetually wet set of cracks sporting a thriving ecosystem including rodents and frogs, and the teflon corner, a section of thin, scary aid climbing. We arrived to these near midnight, ready to be done. Alex soldiered into the wet cracks while I flicked the rope at rats to scare them away from chewing on it. At least this game helped me stay awake... On the next pitch, while standing on some micro cams while admiring "the Boulder Problem" from Freerider to our right, Alex's gear ripped and he took a 30' fall into the darkness. A micronut held after two microcams popped, but he was uninjured. He got back up and finished the pitch. Fixing the rope, all I had to do was ascend and I'd be on the Block where we could eat, sleep, and recover. 

At this point my finger was starting to become a serious concern. As I ascended these last two pitches it was stiff, noticably swollen, and painful. Every time I used it, it would pulse with my heartbeat painfully. I reached the ledge around 1:30 in the morning. We made some chicken broth, ate sandwiches and snacks, and fell into bed quickly. I couldn't sleep. The pain from my finger would get worse every time I lay down, making it impossible to sleep. I took all the Tylenol I carried in my chalkbag for pain relief. I managed to get maybe 30-45 minutes of sleep while sitting upright on the ledge before the early morning light roused Alex awake. "Should we just go?" He asked. Perfect, I was in no mood to wait around.

### Climbing Day 3

I told Alex that morning that my finger wasn't so good and that I might not be able to lead many pitches today. No problem.

On the first pitch of the day, Alex missed a turning point in the route, requiring some mild shenanigans to reverse. We continued on to the base of the Salathe Roof (see picture below, look for our shadows and ropes!). This imposing horizontal roof is one of the most exposed sections of the climb, you have ~3000' feet of thin air beneath your heels, and anything you drop would fall for almost 15 secconds before hitting the ground (neglecting air resistance, of course. This is a physics page.). After this are the stunning Headwall pitches, a veneer of orange granite thinly split by a seam offering just enough to climb. These are the hardest pitches of aid climbing on the route, and Alex being our aid-maestro was happy to dispatch them with style. We arrived at Long ledge, the last stopping point a mere four pitches from the summit. We did in the end find many gallons of stashed water here, but now it was somewhat too late to make a difference. 

![Picture of us climbing the roof and headwall, taken from the meadow through binoculars](/images/Salathe/headwall.jpg)

To get off Long ledge, there are two options. A dangerous aid climb option, or a safe but harder free climbing option. We always intended to opt for the free climbing option. If Alex was our aid-maestro, I was the free-maestro. But circumstances being what they were, Alex tried first. He got out to the first bolt, and started looking for aid placements. He got in a black totem. Still 10' to the next bolt. Hmmmm... "Michael, I think you need to give it a shot". He was fried from carrying us all day. We had no other options, so close to the top we couldn't reasonably turn around. I put on my shoes and mentally prepared, "I'm gonna have to go full Tommy Caldwell on it". Google him if you don't get it.

I tied in and started climbing. I tried to use my finger as little as possible. I got to where Alex had and looked around. The climbing wasn't that bad, 5.11-, but the pain was excruciating. Bolt by bolt I managed to get through it, screaming as I pulled onto a slabby panel with a rest. There was still a 5.9 slabby exit move marked on the topo, and in Yosemite 5.9 slab can be harder than 5.11 sport. It turned out to be my favourite moves of the whole climb, a perfect rail on orange granite leading to a blind toss around a corner. I threw, and grabbed nothing. This was it, I bumped hoping to find something, if not I'd be taking a huge fall, but my fingers closed around a protrusion. All of them, ouch. I grit my teeth and pulled around the corner, hooting and hollering to let Alex know I'd survived. With adrenaline coursing I also aided us through the next pitch, reaching the last alcove of the route.

![View from above the last alcove before the summit](/images/Salathe/last-alcove-view.jpg)

Alex took us up the last two pitches and we topped out around 8pm on June 5th. Elena congratulated us on the radio from the meadow far below, we flicked out headlamps on and off in her direction. 

The descent off the summit is technically difficult and long, and we were exhausted. We opted to wait until morning, and slept on the summit under the canonical bivy tree.

![Our last sleep before descending](/images/Salathe/summit-bivy.jpg)

Alex suggested we try to sleep in as late as possible, after the last few days we surely needed it. But in the morning, I knew I needed to get down ASAP.

### The Days After
I woke up around 6 AM the next morning. My finger hurt. I unwrapped it to change the bandage, and was alarmed at what I saw. The skin was part yellow, part purple. Large blisters full of pus covered the top. I couldn't bend the finger more than a few degrees. Sorry Alex, we need to go.

After I woke him up we gathered our things and began the long walk down. The descent involves several hours of walking carrying your still-heavy bags, before reaching a series of rappels that are probably only about 5, but felt never ending. Then after the rappels you strap the heavy bags on once more and do the last section of walk down to the car park. It took us ~3-4 hours. 

Elena met us at the car park. After collapsing for a moment and chugging all the water she could provide, I dropped my bags with Alex, shared one last celebratory hug, and then got in the car to head straight for the nearest hospital in Mammoth Lakes, ~2 hours away.

When I arrived and was brought back to the ER, the reactions from my nurses told me it was bad. A steadily increasing stream of people filed into my room to see this terribly infected finger. You never want to be an interesting patient in the ER... My doctor, a sage older fellow with the tact to suppress his reaction, sent photos to a hand surgeon who immediately called back. I was in surgery within the hour. 

Infections in the hand can be extremely serious due to **Flexor Tenosynovitis**, inflamation of the tendons casued by infection. It occurs in ~1/10-1/40 hand infections. Not only does it cause swelling, stiffness, and possible loss of the finger, but because of the limited bloodflow in tendons the infection can spread throughout the body rapidly and be resistant to antibiotics. That is why I was rushed into surgery so fast. Because there was no time to have me fast, I was kept awake for the surgery. They asked me what music I wanted (while I was already under anaesthesia) and the only thing I could think to request was Bob Marley, funny enough. After the surgery I got a dose of Fentanyl and passed out.

I spent the next few days in the hospital waiting to see if I would need another round of the same surgery. They took some infected and necrotic tissue to check what bacteria was present, to know what antibiotics I needed. Alex came to visit me in the hospital on his way back home. I was released with oral antibiotics, as they didn't think another surgery would be necessary. Elena and I returned to Yosemite to say goodbye to Carlin. We wanted to take the canonical meadow post-climb photo, but sadly Alex had already left, so this glorious photoshop will have to do:

### Epilogue

At this point the climbing part of the story is over, so let's wrap it up.

A day or two after leaving Yosemite, just as we started to head for the Grand Canyon, my van's transmission died, leaving us stranded in a motel on highway 66. My finger got worse, we changed the bandage to discover the infection had returned, and now we had no way of getting back to Mammoth or any other hospital. We had no choice but to pack up as much of our stuff as we could carry with us and abandon the rest (including the van, RIP), took a Lyft to LA, and caught a flight back East. There were no same-day flights all the way home, so we booked a ticket to Pittsburgh, rented a car there, drove 3 hours home, and I went straight to the hospital. I was back in surgery the next day. Eventually they decided I didn't need a 3rd surgery and released me with painkillers and stronger antibiotics to recover at home, although it was a few more weeks before I was told I was definitely keeping the finger.

Recovery was and is hard. After several months of PT I am regaining use of the finger, but it will be permanently limited. I can do many of the things that are goals for hand-recovery, hold a pencil, write, wear gloves, etc, but things like climbing remain questionable in my recovery.

I don't regret the climb, and I don't think we made mistakes that led to this outcome. Sometimes things just goes south and all you can do is manage it as best you can, that's life.
