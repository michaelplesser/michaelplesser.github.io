I'm a theoretical physics PhD student at Penn State University working with [Dr. Jacob Bourjaily](https://inspirehep.net/authors/1020130).

What I do - the big picture
======
In order to describe relativistic particles like electrons, the necessary language is that of Quantum Field Theory (QFT): it is the only consistent way to combine quantum mechanics and special relativity. For example, QFT is used to compute *scattering amplitudes*, which encode the probabilities for particles to interact with (*i.e.* bump into) each other.

Unfortunately, QFT is famously *hard*, and the textbook approach developed between 1930-1980 (or so) quickly becomes totally impractical or unhelpful for all but the very simplest calculations. Even those "simple" calculations, mind you, are frightfully difficult and yield long expressions. Curiously enough, these could usually be simplified down to astonishingly simple final answers. In retrospect we can appreciate that some of these difficulties were due to choosing *bad* variables, adopting the *wrong* perspectives, and asking *ill-posed* questions. 

In the modern "Amplitudes", or "On-shell" program, one seeks a simplified approach to QFT by choosing *good* variables, emphasizing *physical* principles, and asking only the *right* questions. Doing so we have not only found vastly superior calculational methods (which have allowed us to go far beyond the textbook methods), but those calculations have in turn given a window into an enormously rich space of mathematical structures obfuscated by the textbook approach.

These insights have led to radical combinatoric and geometric re-formulations of amplitudes, revealed new symmetries in our most well-studied theories, probed surprising dualities and relations between ostensibly unrelated theories, and are reinventing how we think about and study QFTs in real time. It is clear that our new understanding is still incomplete, and that much remains to be done. Quaerendo invenietis - there is work to be done!
