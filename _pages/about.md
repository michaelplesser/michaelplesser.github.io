I'm a theoretical physics PhD student at Penn State University working with [Dr. Jacob Bourjaily](https://inspirehep.net/authors/1020130).

What I do - the big picture
======
In order to describe relativistic particles like electrons, the necessary language is that of Quantum Field Theory (QFT). For example, QFT is used to compute **scattering amplitudes**, which encode the probabilities for particles to interact with (*i.e.* bump into) each other.

Unfortunately, QFT is famously *hard*. Textbook approaches prove inadequate for all but the simplest calculations, and even those are difficult and yield complicated answers. Curiously enough, people noticed those could be further simplified down to astonishingly "nice" final answers: **the simplicity of the final answer in contrast with the complexity of the derivation suggests we were doing things wrong**. In retrospect, we can appreciate that these difficulties were often due to choosing *bad* variables or adopting the *wrong* perspectives. 

In the modern **Amplitudes** or **on-shell program**, one seeks a simplified approach to QFT by choosing *good* variables and emphasizing *physical* principles. In doing so we have not only found vastly superior calculational methods, but those calculations have given us a window into an enormously rich space of mathematical structures obfuscated by the textbook approach.

These insights have led to radical re-formulations of amplitudes, revealed new symmetries in our most well-studied theories, probed dualities between ostensibly unrelated theories, and are reinventing how we think about QFTs in real time. It is clear that our new understanding is still incomplete, and that much remains to be understood. There is work to be done!
